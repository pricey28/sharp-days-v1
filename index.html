In the moment

Toolkit

Clear thinking sits at the top of the curve. Feeling flat and feeling wound up both pull you off it, in opposite directions. Where are you now?

Lift your alertness

Move firstStand up and walk, even for two minutes. A 10–20 minute brisk walk sharpens thinking for an hour or two afterwards.
Get into the conversation earlyAsk a question or restate the problem out loud. Speaking warms up retrieval faster than listening.
Caffeine, if it's before 2 pmAny later and it can cost you tonight's sleep and tomorrow's sharpness.
Check the basicsWater, and something to eat if it's been a while.
Move it to your peakIf you can, put demanding conversations at your sharpest time of day. Trends will show when that is.

Stay where you are

Lead with your pointUse the structure below so you don't have to invent one on the spot.
Keep attention outwardOn the question and the person, not on how well you're doing.
Note what got you hereLog today tonight. Good days are where your recipe comes from.

Settle your system

One or two long exhalesBreathe in, top up with a short second breath, then breathe out slowly. Nobody notices. Practise with the pacer below.
Reframe the arousalTell yourself "my body is getting ready". This tends to work better than forcing calm.
Coach yourself by name"Tom, just give the main point." Third-person self-talk reduces distress under social pressure.
Point your attention outwardSelf-monitoring uses the working memory you need to think. Focus on the question and the person.

Buy a few seconds

"Good question. Let me think about that for a second."

"So you're asking whether…"

"There are two parts to that."

"Let me start with the main point."

Unsure of the facts? Say what you know, and what you'd need to check.

When a word won't come

Describe it instead: "the test that measures…"
Swap in a simpler word and keep going. People judge ideas, not vocabulary.
A brief silent pause reads as composed. Visible searching draws attention.
Stop forcing it. The word usually turns up a minute later.

Before a big conversation

Say your key terms and main point out loud. Recently used words come back more easily.
Walk briskly for 10 minutes if you have the time.
Anxious about it? Write your worries down for a few minutes first.
Two long exhales on the way in.

Breathe

Two breaths in through the nose, one long breath out. Five minutes a day lowers baseline anxiety. One breath helps in the moment.

Ready when you are

Out loud

Practice

Rehearsed skills hold up on off days. Pick a prompt, start the timer and answer out loud. About three short sessions a week is plenty.

Daily foundations

Wake at the same time every day, weekends included.
Put demanding conversations at your sharpest time of day.
Walk briskly for 10–20 minutes before something important.
Group similar work, and jot down where you left off before switching.
Caffeine before 2 pm, alcohol light on work nights, and stay hydrated.
Optional: 10 minutes of mindfulness a day.

See your GP sooner if

Word-finding or slowness becomes more frequent, starts affecting work, or comes with any other new change. Don't wait for the log to finish.

The evidence behind this
  • Arnsten 2009, Nature Reviews Neuroscience. Stress chemistry past its optimal level impairs prefrontal reasoning and working memory. Too little arousal impairs it too.
  • Dickerson & Kemeny 2004, Psychological Bulletin. Being evaluated by others is one of the strongest triggers of a cortisol response.
  • Phillips et al. 2017, Scientific Reports. Irregular sleep timing is linked to poorer performance, independent of total sleep.
  • May & Hasher 1998. People perform better at their body clock's peak time of day.
  • Leroy 2009. Switching tasks leaves attention residue that slows the next task.
  • Chang et al. 2012, Brain Research. A single bout of exercise briefly improves executive function.
  • Wittbrodt & Millard-Stafford 2018. Losing 2% or more of body water impairs attention and executive function.
  • Jha et al. 2010; Mrazek et al. 2013. Short mindfulness training protected or improved working memory.
  • Balban et al. 2023, Cell Reports Medicine. Five minutes a day of exhale-focused breathing reduced anxiety more than mindfulness meditation.
  • Jamieson et al. 2010, 2012. Reappraising arousal as helpful improved test scores and cardiovascular responses.
  • Kross et al. 2014, JPSP. Third-person self-talk reduced distress during a social stress task.
  • Smith, Floerke & Thomas 2016, Science. Material learned through self-testing held up under acute stress.
  • Ramirez & Beilock 2011, Science. Writing about worries before a high-pressure test improved performance.
  • Burke et al. 1991; James & Burke 2000. Tip-of-the-tongue states come from weakened links to a word's sound. Recent use and hearing part of the word help.

Most of these effects are modest and come from lab studies. They add up.
